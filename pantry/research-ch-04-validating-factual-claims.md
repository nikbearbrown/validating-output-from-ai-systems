# Research: Chapter 04 — Validating Factual Claims
## Validating Output from AI Systems
**Chapter one-line:** Apply RAG grounding, citation-existence + claim-support checking, and claim decomposition — and explain why RAG shifts hallucination into retrieval error rather than eliminating it.
**Research date:** 2026-05-29

## 1. Primary Sources

### Foundational papers and texts

- **Garfield, E. (1955). "Citation Indexes for Science: A New Dimension in Documentation through Association of Ideas." *Science* 122(3159), 108–111.** The intellectual root of the chapter's core move: treat a citation as a checkable object, not decoration. Garfield's citation index made "does this reference exist, and does it actually support the claim attached to it?" a mechanical, indexable question decades before LLMs. The chapter's citation-existence check is, literally, a single-document citation lookup. Foundational; cite for the lineage.

- **Vannevar Bush (1945). "As We May Think." *The Atlantic*.** The deeper ancestor (it inspired Garfield): the idea that knowledge is a graph of linked, traceable claims. Useful framing for "grounding" — a claim with no traceable source is unverifiable by construction. Cite for framing only.

- **Lewis, P. et al. (2020). "Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks." NeurIPS 2020.** The canonical RAG paper — the technique the chapter both recommends and criticizes. Establishes the mechanism: retrieve documents, condition generation on them. The chapter's argument is that this *grounds* generation but introduces a new dependency (retrieval quality) — it relocates the failure, it doesn't remove it. (Cite as the technique's origin; verify exact venue/year if printing.)

### Key empirical cases

- **Vázquez, R. et al. (2025). "SemEval-2025 Task 3: Mu-SHROOM, the Multilingual Shared Task on Hallucinations and Related Observable Overgeneration Mistakes." arXiv:2504.11975.** *VERIFIED.* The chapter's anchor for **span-level** hallucination — the field has moved from "is this answer hallucinated?" (binary) to "*which spans* are unsupported?" (labeling). Frames detection as a **span-labeling task**: mark the substrings of generated text not supported by a reference document (Wikipedia). **14 languages** (Arabic, Basque, Catalan, Chinese, Czech, English, Farsi, Finnish, French, German, Hindi, Italian, Spanish, Swedish); dataset spans **38 LLMs**; **2,618 submissions from 43 teams**. This is the chapter's evidence that hallucination is now localized to spans, which is what makes claim-level validation tractable.

- **Ayala, O.M. & Béchard, P. (2024). "Reducing Hallucination in Structured Outputs via Retrieval-Augmented Generation." NAACL 2024 (Industry Track), pp. 228–238. arXiv:2404.08189.** *VERIFIED.* The "RAG reduces hallucination" evidence — but in *structured* output (workflows from NL requirements), a deployed ServiceNow system. Reports RAG "significantly reduces hallucination" and improves out-of-domain generalization. Important nuance for the chapter: the gains are clearest where the output has a checkable structure, which connects to Ch. 6. Cite as the grounded reduction case.

- **Xiong, X. et al. (2025). "MEGA-RAG: a retrieval-augmented generation framework with multi-evidence guided answer refinement for mitigating hallucinations of LLMs in public health." *Frontiers in Public Health* 13:1635381 (PMC12540348; PubMed 41132171).** *VERIFIED.* The ~40% figure is confirmed: MEGA-RAG achieves a **>40% reduction in hallucination rate** vs. baselines (incl. standalone LLM and standard RAG), with accuracy 0.7913 / F1 0.7904. Mechanism: multi-source retrieval (FAISS dense + BM25 + biomedical KG) + cross-encoder reranker + discrepancy-aware refinement. Peer-reviewed (Frontiers), high-stakes domain — exactly the chapter's "RAG helps but the work shifts to retrieval quality" exhibit.

- **Mata v. Avianca, Inc., 678 F. Supp. 3d 443 (S.D.N.Y. 2023).** *VERIFIED — case name and facts confirmed.* The legal fabricated-cases failure. Plaintiff's lawyers filed a brief with **fake precedents generated by ChatGPT** (fictitious airlines, fabricated quotations, fake internal citations). When challenged, they doubled down — ChatGPT "assured" them the cases existed in Westlaw/LexisNexis. **Judge P. Kevin Castel sanctioned the lawyers and their firm $5,000** (decision June 22, 2023), calling one fabricated analysis "gibberish." The textbook-perfect demonstration of two distinct failures: (1) **citation existence** (the cases don't exist) and (2) the fluency trap (the fabrications were plausible enough to file). Verified primary: the S.D.N.Y. opinion; Wikipedia/CNN/Seyfarth corroborate.

- **Citation-faithfulness corpus evidence (2024–2026).** Multiple studies separate **citation existence** from **claim support**: across ~17,443 generated citations, **no model exceeded a 0.475 existence rate** (most cited references don't exist); and even among *existing* citations, **50–90% of responses are not fully supported** by the sources cited, with ~30% of individual statements unsupported. Formalizes the chapter's key distinction: *citation exists ≠ citation supports the claim*, and *faithful ≠ correct*. (See sourcing notes: some of these surfaced as future-dated arXiv IDs — flag.)

## 2. The Core Concept — State of the Field

### What is settled
- **Hallucination is localizable to spans, not just whole answers** (Mu-SHROOM 2025). This is what makes claim-level validation possible: you decompose an answer into atomic claims and check each.
- **RAG reduces hallucination but does not eliminate it** (Ayala & Béchard 2024; MEGA-RAG >40% reduction). The reduction is real and replicated across domains.
- **Citation existence and claim support are different checks.** A reference can exist and not support the claim; it can support the claim and be factually wrong; it can be fabricated entirely (Mata v. Avianca). The corpus evidence (existence ≤0.475; 50–90% unsupported) settles that both checks are necessary.
- **Fluency defeats reviewers in factual domains specifically.** The Mata lawyers filed fabrications because they read as real — the book's central "plausible ≠ correct" claim, documented in court.

### What is disputed
- **How much of the residual error after RAG is retrieval error vs. generation error.** RAG shifts the failure into the retriever (wrong/missing/outdated document → confidently wrong answer grounded in the wrong source). The exact split is task- and corpus-dependent and contested.
- **Whether automated claim-support checking (NLI/entailment, LLM-judge) is reliable enough to certify high-stakes claims.** Existence is mechanically checkable (does the DOI/URL resolve?); *support* requires judgment, and judging support with another LLM reintroduces the fluency problem (cross-ref Ch. 8).
- **Span-labeling agreement** is imperfect even among humans for borderline "partially supported" spans — the three-way full/partial/none distinction is fuzzy.

### What has changed recently (last 5 years)
- Detection moved from **binary answer-level** to **span-level** labeling (SHROOM 2024 → Mu-SHROOM 2025, 14 languages).
- RAG went from research technique (Lewis et al. 2020) to default production grounding, and the discourse matured from "RAG fixes hallucination" to "RAG relocates it into retrieval."
- The fabricated-citation failure went from hypothetical to **sanctioned courtroom fact** (Mata v. Avianca 2023) and spawned a wave of citation-verification benchmarks (2024–2026).

## 3. Application Domain Examples
1. **Citation-existence gate (mechanical):** resolve every DOI/URL/case cite; reject the document if any fails to resolve. Would have caught Mata v. Avianca outright — the cases don't exist.
2. **Claim-support check (judgmental):** decompose the answer into atomic claims; for each, retrieve the cited span and run entailment (does the source *say* this?). Catches the "real citation, wrong claim" 50–90%-unsupported class.
3. **RAG retrieval-error failure:** the model answers confidently and cites a real document — but the retriever pulled an outdated or off-topic passage, so the grounded answer is wrong. Guard: validate retrieval (relevance/recency), not just generation. The failure moved upstream.
4. **High-stakes public-health RAG (MEGA-RAG):** >40% fewer hallucinations than standalone LLM — but the residual 60% still needs expert review before clinical/policy use. The data justifies "RAG then expert," not "RAG instead of expert."
5. **Span-level review UI:** highlight unsupported spans (Mu-SHROOM style) so a reviewer reads the suspect substrings, not the whole fluent answer — a direct counter to the fluency trap.

## 4. The Book's Thesis Connection
Factual claims are the chapter where the thesis's *both halves* are sharpest. **Where ground truth is mechanically available, validation works:** a citation either resolves or it doesn't — citation-existence checking is as deterministic as a compiler, and it would have stopped Mata v. Avianca cold. **Where ground truth isn't mechanical, validation degrades:** *claim support* requires judgment (does this source actually back this sentence?), and *factual accuracy in a novel domain* may have no retrievable ground truth at all. RAG is the chapter's perfect illustration of "validation relocates rather than dissolves the problem": it grounds generation in retrieved evidence (good — reduces hallucination 40%+), but it makes the answer only as correct as the retrieval, so the failure mode *moves* from confabulation to retrieval error. And the fluency clause is on full display in court: the Mata fabrications were filed precisely because they read as real. The discipline the chapter teaches is the book's: deterministic checks first (does the citation exist?), then layered judgmental checks (does it support the claim?), then — in high-stakes domains — a human expert who owns the residual risk.

## 6. Pedagogical Delivery Research
- **Lead with Mata v. Avianca** — a real sanction, a named judge, a $5,000 fine, "gibberish" in the record. It makes "citations that don't exist" visceral and dislodges the misconception that fluent legal prose implies real law.
- **Teach the two checks as separate gates:** existence (mechanical, do it first, cheap) then support (judgmental, expensive). Have the reader run each on an LLM answer and find a citation that exists but doesn't support its claim — the 50–90%-unsupported finding made concrete.
- **Demonstrate RAG-relocates-the-failure:** give the model a deliberately wrong retrieved passage and watch it produce a confident, well-cited, *wrong* answer. The point lands harder than a definition: grounding moved the error, it didn't remove it.
- **Use span-highlighting** as the review affordance — readers should learn to read the suspect spans, not the fluent whole.

## 8. Open Questions and Research Gaps
- **How reliably can claim-support checking be automated** without reintroducing the fluency problem (LLM judging LLM)? Existence is solved; support is not. Cross-ref Ch. 8.
- **What fraction of post-RAG error is retrieval error vs. generation error**, and how to attribute it automatically? No standard decomposition.
- **Factual validation in genuinely novel domains** (no retrievable ground truth) — the hard residual the chapter must admit is unsolved; defers to Ch. 12 (scalable oversight).
- **Cross-lingual hallucination detection** — Mu-SHROOM shows performance varies sharply by language; low-resource languages are under-served.
- **Span-label agreement on "partially supported"** — the full/partial/none boundary is fuzzy even for humans; reliable three-way labeling is open.
